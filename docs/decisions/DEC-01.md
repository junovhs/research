# DEC-01: Dig needles, not random samples; ask unasked questions; minimal paperwork

## Context

The reset plan screened a random 10k-row Gaia sample with heavy process documents. The owner wants to explore and ask questions, not maintain bureaucracy.

## Decision

Research is a pencil-beam dig: three needles (calibration, exploration, confirmation), dug one public survey layer at a time and cross-matched. Questions come from data-first probes plus an Alien-inspired map of coherent but rarely asked combinations of what the needle measures. Each look gets one LOG.md entry; Ishoo holds plans and issues; no new planning documents.

## Why

Owner: 'picked a spot in the sky, and kept zooming... cataloguing everything we find along that needle of depth'; 'I don't want to get bogged down in plans and documentation bureaucracy'; and wants to map what 'nobody has asked... or compared'. A needle keeps data bounded for consumer hardware, makes cross-survey disagreements visible, and gives a sky-separated holdout that is more independent than a random row split.

## Alternatives rejected

Random or stratified all-sky catalogue sample (the previous plan): rejected as less coherent and with a weaker holdout. Only famous deep fields: rejected for exploration because they are the most-studied patches of sky; kept as the calibration needle. A single needle: rejected because one line of sight produces flukes (e.g. Broadhurst et al. 1990 periodicity).

## Consequences

Every comparison must account for each survey's detection limit (Malmquist bias). Confirmation needle data stays untouched until a claim is frozen. Availability scores measure gaps in our literature query, not what all scientists have thought.
